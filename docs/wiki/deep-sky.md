## 🌌 Galaxies

### Galaxy types

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Spiral galaxy** | Flat disk with arms winding out from a bright central bulge. Example: Andromeda Galaxy |
| **Barred spiral galaxy** | Spiral arms starting from the ends of a straight bar of stars through the center. Example: the Milky Way |
| **Grand-design spiral** | Two long, clear, well-defined arms |
| **Flocculent spiral** | Many short, patchy arm pieces instead of a few long arms |
| **Elliptical galaxy** | Smooth ball or egg of stars, from round to stretched. Example: Messier 87 |
| **Giant elliptical galaxy** | Huge smooth ball of stars at the heart of a galaxy cluster. Example: Messier 87 |
| **Lenticular galaxy** | A disk and bulge with no spiral arms. Example: Sombrero Galaxy |
| **Irregular galaxy** | No clear shape |
| **Magellanic spiral** | A small lopsided galaxy with one main arm. Example: Large Magellanic Cloud |
| **Dwarf elliptical galaxy** | Small smooth ball of old stars |
| **Dwarf spheroidal galaxy** | Faint, small, loose ball of stars, often circling a big galaxy |
| **Dwarf irregular galaxy** | Small and shapeless, with gas and young stars |
| **Ultra-diffuse galaxy** | As wide as a big galaxy but very faint, with few stars |
| **Ring galaxy** | A ring of stars around an empty center, made when one galaxy punches through another. Example: Cartwheel Galaxy |
| **Polar-ring galaxy** | A ring of gas and stars circling over the galaxy's poles |
| **Starburst galaxy** | Any shape, forming stars far faster than normal. Example: Messier 82 |
| **Active galaxy** | Any shape, with a bright feeding black hole in its core |
| **Quasar host galaxy** | A galaxy whose central black hole outshines all its stars |
| **Radio galaxy** | A galaxy firing huge jets from its central black hole, glowing in radio light |
| **Interacting galaxies** | Two galaxies pulling each other out of shape, with tails of stars between them |

### Extra galaxy types

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Compact elliptical (cE)** | A small, very dense ball of stars |
| **cD galaxy** | A giant elliptical with a huge faint halo, at the heart of a galaxy cluster |
| **Blue compact dwarf** | A small galaxy bursting with young blue stars |
| **Green pea galaxy** | A tiny, very bright galaxy forming stars at a furious rate |
| **Jellyfish galaxy** | A galaxy falling through a cluster, its gas stripped out into long tentacles where new stars form |
| **Peculiar galaxy** | A galaxy that fits no normal type, usually bent by a collision or a close pass |
| **Dark galaxy** *(predicted by science)* | A galaxy of gas and dark matter with few or no stars |

### Real examples

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **The Milky Way** | Our home galaxy, our own star is in one of its arms |
| **Andromeda Galaxy** | The nearest big galaxy, heading toward the Milky Way |
| **Large Magellanic Cloud** | A satellite galaxy of the Milky Way |
| **Messier 87** | Home of the first black hole ever photographed |

### Parts a galaxy is built from

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Central bulge** | A thick bright crowd of mostly old stars at the center |
| **Bar** | A straight band of stars through the center |
| **Disk** | The thin flat plane holding most stars, gas and dust |
| **Spiral arms** | Bright bands of young stars and gas winding out from the center |
| **Halo** | A faint round cloud of old stars and globular clusters around the whole galaxy |
| **Central black hole** | A supermassive black hole at the heart of most big galaxies |
| **Globular clusters** | Tight balls of ancient stars orbiting in the halo |
| **Dust lanes** | Dark streaks of dust along the arms |
| **Star-forming regions** | Glowing nebulas where new stars are born, mostly in the arms |
| **Tidal tails** | Long streams of stars pulled out by a passing galaxy |

### Structures between galaxies

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Galaxy pair** | Two galaxies bound together by gravity |
| **Galaxy group** | A few dozen galaxies bound together. Example: the Local Group, home of the Milky Way and Andromeda |
| **Galaxy cluster** | Hundreds to thousands of galaxies bound together, filled with hot gas |
| **Supercluster** | Groups and clusters strung together over vast distances |
| **Cosmic filament** | Long threads of galaxies and gas, the strands of the cosmic web |
| **Cosmic void** | Huge nearly empty spaces between the filaments |

**How it works in the game:**

- Galaxies run on a scale from smooth ellipticals, to lenticulars, to tight spirals, to loose spirals, to irregulars; any of them can be barred, ringed or peculiar
- Every galaxy is generated live from the world seed; nothing is pre-built
- The player picks Small, Medium, Large or Massive; size sets how many star systems the galaxy holds, every one fully simulated all the time
- The type decides which parts are used; spirals get arms, ellipticals don't, irregulars get patches
- Gas-rich galaxies keep forming new stars; gas-poor galaxies hold mostly old stars
- Young blue stars, nebulas and star-forming regions sit in the arms; old red stars sit in the bulge and halo
- Big galaxies get a supermassive black hole at the center; some feed it and become active
- A world can hold more than one galaxy; extra galaxies sit in pairs, groups or clusters along filaments, with voids between
- Galaxies that pass close pull out tidal tails, may trigger a starburst, and can merge into one
- Big galaxies are circled by dwarf and satellite galaxies
- Only our own galaxy is the Milky Way and our own star is never named here; generated ones get generated names

## 🪐 Star systems

### Kinds of systems

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Usual system** | Small rocky planets close in, gas and ice giants further out, belts and an icy outer shell. Example: the Solar System |
| **Compact system** | Many rocky planets packed tightly close to a dim star. Example: TRAPPIST-1 |
| **Hot-Jupiter system** | A gas giant orbiting very close to its star, usually with few close neighbors. Example: 51 Pegasi |
| **Crowded system** | Many planets of every size. Example: Kepler-90 |
| **Circumbinary system** | Planets orbiting both stars at once. Example: Kepler-16 |
| **Wide binary system** | Each star with its own planets. Example: Alpha Centauri |
| **Triple-star system** | Planets around one star, the others far off. Example: Alpha Centauri (with Proxima Centauri) |
| **Red-dwarf system** | Planets close in, often locked with one face to the star. Example: Proxima Centauri |
| **Giant-free system** | Only small rocky planets |
| **Young system** | A star still wrapped in its dust and gas disk, planets still forming |
| **Debris-disk system** | A grown star with a wide belt of dust and rock left over |
| **White-dwarf system** | The survivors of a dead one-solar-mass star, often with rubble falling in |
| **Pulsar system** | Planets circling a spinning dead star. Example: PSR B1257+12, home of the first planets ever found outside the Solar System |
| **Starless planet** | A rogue planet with no star, sometimes with moons |

### Zones a system is built from

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Inner rocky zone** | Close to the star, too hot for ice; small rocky planets form here |
| **Habitable zone** | The band where liquid water can last on a planet's surface; its distance depends on how bright the star is |
| **Frost line** | The distance where ice can form; beyond it, giant planets can grow |
| **Asteroid belt** | A ring of rocks between the rocky planets and the giants |
| **Giant zone** | Gas giants and ice giants, each with many moons and often rings |
| **Kuiper belt** | Icy bodies and dwarf planets beyond the last giant |
| **Scattered disk** | Icy bodies thrown into long, tilted orbits beyond the Kuiper belt |
| **Oort cloud** | A huge round shell of comets far out around the star, reaching out a light-year or more |
| **Trojan points** | Spots ahead of and behind a planet in its orbit where rocks collect |
| **Ring zone** | Close to a giant planet, where moons can't hold together and rings form |
| **Resonant-chain system** | Planets whose orbits lock together in a steady beat, each lap timed to the next. Example: TRAPPIST-1, TOI-178 |
| **Detached objects** | Icy bodies on huge lonely orbits beyond the Kuiper belt. Example: Sedna |
| **Heliosphere** | The bubble the star's wind blows around the whole system; its edge is where interstellar space begins |

**How it works in the game:**

- Young planets often drift into locked resonant chains; most chains later break apart
- A moon can only stay with a planet inside that planet's sphere of pull; too far out and the star takes it
- Every system is generated live from the world seed; nothing is pre-built
- The star is picked first; its type sets where the habitable zone and frost line fall
- Rocky planets mostly form inside the frost line, giants outside it, belts sit in the gaps, and an Oort cloud surrounds it all
- Giant planets can wander inward after forming, making hot Jupiters and scattering smaller planets and comets
- Giants collect many moons; rocky planets have few or none; big impacts can make a large moon
- Comets from the Oort cloud and Kuiper belt sometimes fall inward, and some hit planets
- In two- and three-star systems, planets orbit one star or circle the whole group, never in between
- Life is most likely in the habitable zone, but ice-shell moons and other strange worlds can hold life too
- Young systems still have dust disks and forming planets; old systems have settled orbits
- Only our own system is the Solar System and our own star is never named here; generated ones get generated names

|---|---|

### Giants and supergiants

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Red giant** | Late life of a one-solar-mass star |
| **Red supergiant** | Late life of a heavy star. Example: Betelgeuse |
| **Blue supergiant** | Example: Rigel |
| **Wolf-Rayet star** | Heavy star blowing off its outer layers |

### Young and failed stars

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Protostar** | A star still forming inside a gas cloud |
| **T Tauri star** | A young one-solar-mass star, not yet steady |
| **Brown dwarf** | Too light to burn like a true star |

### Dead and strange stars

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **White dwarf** | The leftover core of a one-solar-mass star |
| **Neutron star** | The crushed core left by a supernova |
| **Pulsar** | A spinning neutron star sweeping beams through space |
| **Magnetar** | A neutron star with an enormous magnetic field |
| **Stellar black hole** | The collapsed core of a very heavy star |
| **Cepheid variable** | A giant that swells and shrinks on a steady beat |

### Luminosity classes

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Hypergiant (class 0 / Ia+)** | The brightest, heaviest stars, losing mass in huge winds. Example: Eta Carinae |
| **Supergiant (class I)** | Enormous, very bright stars near the end of their lives |
| **Bright giant (class II)** | Between giants and supergiants |
| **Giant (class III)** | Example: Arcturus |
| **Subgiant (class IV)** | A star just starting to swell into a giant |
| **Main-sequence star (class V)** | A star steadily burning hydrogen, like our own |
| **Subdwarf (class VI)** | Dimmer than a main-sequence star of the same color |

### Extra star types

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Carbon star (class C)** | A cool giant whose air is rich in carbon |
| **S-type star** | A cool giant between ordinary red giants and carbon stars |
| **L-type brown dwarf** | The warmest brown dwarfs, with metal-hydride clouds |
| **T-type brown dwarf** | Cooler brown dwarfs with methane in their air |
| **Y-type brown dwarf** | The coldest brown dwarfs, barely warmer than a planet |
| **RR Lyrae variable** | An old pulsing star that brightens and dims in less than a day |
| **Mira variable** | A pulsing red giant that brightens and dims over months |

**How it works in the game:**

- Color follows surface temperature, from red (coolest) to blue (hottest)
- Heavier stars burn hotter and die sooner, lighter stars burn cooler and live longer
- When a galaxy is generated, red dwarfs are by far the most common and blue stars the rarest
- Every generated star picks its values from inside its type's ranges, from the world seed
- Our own star is never named here; every star is a star

## ⚫ Black holes and quasars

### Kinds of black hole

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Stellar-mass black hole** | The most common kind, often found feeding on a companion star. Example: V404 Cygni |
| **Intermediate-mass black hole** | Rare and hard to find; candidates are still debated. Example: the remnant of the merger GW190521, about 142 solar masses |
| **Supermassive black hole** | Example: Sagittarius A*, about 4 million solar masses |
| **Ultramassive black hole** |  |
| **Primordial black hole** *(predicted by science)* | Never confirmed; could come in any size |
| **Spinning black hole** | A black hole that rotates, dragging space around with it; it has an ergosphere |
| **Binary black hole** | Two black holes orbiting each other, spiraling closer until they merge |

### Parts

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Singularity** | The center, where all the mass is crushed |
| **Event horizon** | The edge past which nothing, not even light, can escape; bigger mass, bigger horizon |
| **Photon sphere** | A shell where light can circle the black hole |
| **Shadow** | The dark disk seen against glowing gas, as in the first photo of a black hole |
| **Ergosphere** | The region around a spinning black hole where space itself is dragged around; energy can be taken from it |
| **Accretion disk** | A spinning disk of gas heated so hot it shines in X-rays |
| **Corona** | Super-hot gas above the disk |
| **Jets** | Beams of matter fired from the poles at nearly the speed of light, shaped by magnetic fields |
| **Radio lobes** | Huge clouds where jets crash into the gas around a galaxy |
| **Dusty torus** | A thick ring of dust around an active galaxy's core that can hide the center from some angles |

### Active galaxy cores

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Quasar** | A feeding supermassive black hole so bright it outshines its whole galaxy; seen far across the universe |
| **Blazar** | An active core with its jet pointed straight at the viewer; very bright and flickering |
| **BL Lacertae object** | A blazar with almost no lines in its light |
| **Seyfert galaxy, type 1** | A spiral galaxy with a bright core; fast-moving gas is seen directly |
| **Seyfert galaxy, type 2** | Like type 1, but the dusty torus hides the fast-moving gas. Example: NGC 1068 (Messier 77) |
| **Radio galaxy** | Usually an elliptical galaxy with huge radio lobes fed by jets. Example: Messier 87 |
| **LINER** | A dim active core, the faint end of active galaxies |
| **Radio-loud core** | An active core with strong jets and lobes |
| **Radio-quiet core** | An active core without strong jets |

### Events

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Tidal disruption** | A star wanders too close and is torn apart, flaring brightly as it falls in |
| **Spaghettification** | The stretching of anything falling toward a black hole |
| **Black hole merger** | Two black holes collide and send out gravitational waves. Example: GW190521 |
| **Gravitational waves** | Ripples in space from black holes and neutron stars colliding |
| **Feeding flare** | A quiet black hole lights up when gas falls in |

### Real examples

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Sagittarius A*** | About 4 million solar masses, no detected jet |
| **M87*** | The first black hole ever photographed (2019), with a jet about 5,000 light-years long |
| **V404 Cygni** | Feeding on a one-solar-mass companion star |
| **NGC 1068** | Where astronomers found the hidden fast-moving gas behind the dust |

**How it works in the game:**

- Every black hole is generated live from the world seed; nothing is pre-built
- Mass decides the kind: stellar from dead heavy stars, intermediate from mergers, supermassive at galaxy centers
- A black hole with gas falling in grows a hot disk and may fire jets; a starved one stays dark
- One feeding core looks different from different angles: a blazar seen down the jet, a quasar or Seyfert 1 seen from above, a Seyfert 2 seen through the dust
- Energy can be drawn from a spinning black hole's ergosphere; advanced civilizations can tap it
- Close in, tides tear apart stars, planets and ships; jets sterilize anything in their path
- Primordial black holes are hypothetical; the game may generate them, marked as such

## 🌫️ Nebulas

### The five main kinds

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Emission nebula (H II region)** | Gas lit up from inside by hot young stars; where new stars are being born. Example: the Orion Nebula |
| **Reflection nebula** | Dust that only reflects the light of nearby stars. Example: NGC 1999 |
| **Dark nebula** | Dust so thick it blocks the light behind it. Example: the Horsehead Nebula, the Coalsack |
| **Planetary nebula** | The glowing shell a dying one-solar-mass star throws off; nothing to do with planets. Example: the Helix Nebula |
| **Supernova remnant** | The wreck of an exploded star, a shock wave sweeping up gas as it spreads. Example: the Crab Nebula, from a star seen exploding in 1054 |

### Star-forming structures

| | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Molecular cloud** | A huge, cold, dark cloud of gas and dust, the raw material for new stars |
| **Giant molecular cloud** | A molecular cloud big enough to build thousands of stars |
| **Bok globule** | A small, dense, dark knot of dust where one or a few stars may form |
| **Pillar of creation** | A tall column of dust and gas carved by the light of nearby hot stars, with new stars forming inside |
| **Herbig-Haro object** | Bright knots where jets from a newborn star crash into the gas around it |
| **Protoplanetary nebula** | A short-lived cloud around a dying giant, just before it becomes a planetary nebula |
| **Stellar wind bubble** | A hollow blown in the gas by a strong star's wind |

**How it works in the game:**

- Every nebula is generated live from the world seed; nothing is pre-built
- A cloud glows red if hot stars light it from inside, blue if it reflects starlight, and black if there's no light behind it
- Molecular clouds and their globules and pillars collapse into new stars and solar systems over time
- Dying one-solar-mass stars leave planetary nebulas; exploding heavy stars leave supernova remnants that seed new clouds with heavy elements
- Star-forming nebulas sit mostly in a galaxy's spiral arms
- Thick nebulas block sight and slow travel; they are also rich in gas resources
